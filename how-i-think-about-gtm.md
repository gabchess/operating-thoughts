# How I think about GTM engineering

Fifteen years in marketing taught me to ask why someone would care about a product. I've worked on positioning and spent years researching audiences. As a ghostwriter, I had to understand someone else's point of view well enough to write in their voice.

For the past five years, I've been applying that experience to GTM engineering and AI systems. I work with the people who will use the system, learn where their process gets difficult, and build around that. The marketing experience helps me decide what the workflow should do and how to judge what comes out.

## Where I start

I like to see someone do the work before discussing a solution. A person can describe a process as simple and then pause to find the right document. Watching them makes the problem much easier to understand.

I ask what they need to decide at each step. If a field has no effect on that decision, I look at whether it needs to be there. A clear label can save an explanation. Sometimes the useful change is filling in information the team already has.

This carries into product design. I want a new user to understand the next action, including when the page has no data yet. I pay attention to where they hesitate, because I already know the interface too well to notice every problem myself.

## The work between teams

Marketing, sales, and data each have information the others need. Customer questions can improve positioning. Research can help sales understand why an account is relevant. A campaign report should help the team decide what to change next.

I get interested when that information stops moving. Someone ends up copying a row between tools, rebuilding a report, or asking a question that was answered in another conversation. Those repeated handoffs are often where I can make a useful change.

With [Prospector](https://github.com/gabchess/prospector), I worked on the sequence from finding companies to preparing an outbound list. The source choice matters before enrichment begins. I want to know why a company qualifies and what evidence supports spending time on it. A person then reviews the proposed outreach.

[Launch Factory](https://github.com/gabchess/launch-factory) addresses another recurring task. A release already has notes and footage, yet someone has to rebuild the launch package. I want the drafts and campaign plan to reach the reviewer together, with the facts available to check. Producing another document helps only if it makes that person's work easier.

## What marketing brings to the build

I need enough technical understanding to build the workflow and enough knowledge of the business to question its purpose. If I automate a weak process, the team still has to deal with what comes out of it.

My marketing background helps here. I've had to explain technical products to people with different reasons to care. That experience affects the questions I ask during discovery and the details I keep in the output.

I work through those choices with the team. They know the exceptions and the parts of the process that depend on judgment. I can turn that knowledge into requirements, build a first version, and watch what happens when they use it. Their feedback changes the next version.

I also want someone else to be able to run the workflow. Clear setup instructions, useful error messages, and a saved example make that possible. I include them in the work I hand over.

## Writing for the place people will see it

I approach X as a conversation already in progress. A short take over a screenshot can be enough if the observation is clear and the timing fits. I read the replies to understand what people picked up on.

With short-form video, I care about the opening frame. I want the person or action that makes the video interesting to be visible early, so the viewer has a reason to stay.

Instagram makes me think about composition and how a body of work feels when someone visits the profile. I use Reels to reach people and the feed to give them a sense of the person or product. A carousel needs enough substance to make each swipe worthwhile.

I've spent years in crypto communities, where shared language can help an idea travel quickly. When I want a product to reach people outside those communities, I explain what it helps them do in words they already use. The audience and the job shape the message.

## What I look for afterward

I care about what happens once someone encounters the product. The first email and the error message are both part of their experience with the brand. A campaign can set an expectation that the product then has to meet.

Metrics help me see parts of that experience. At Octant, I built analytics and cohort analysis that informed the V1-to-V2 migration campaign. I also worked on social strategy and distribution with other people on the team. The numbers matter alongside an honest account of my contribution.

I want people to feel informed and interested enough to come back. Some of that appears in a report; some comes through a conversation or a question they ask after using the product. I use that feedback when deciding what to change next.
